Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS)

A pilot study of social maturity and adaptive behaviour in children aged 1–15 in Bangalore, using the Indian adaptation of the VSMS. Ten parents rated their child’s everyday skills across eight areas, including self-help, communication, locomotion and socialization. I scored each child’s Social Age and Social Quotient (SQ = Social Age ÷ Chronological Age × 100) by hand.

  • 5 of 10 children showed a mild social deficit, 2 were below average and 3 were average.
  • Delays appeared most in self-help, communication and socialization.
  • The scale took parents 15–25 minutes and was easy to understand and score.
  • Limits: small convenience sample, one informant (parent) and cultural differences in expectations of independence.

Parental Support Scale (PSS-NA)

A case report on parental support among ten parents of children aged 10 and under in Bangalore, using the self-report PSS-NA, which covers three dimensions: interest, behaviour and resource provision.

  • Support varied widely: some parents scored consistently high, some low, and many showed mixed patterns.
  • Interest and resource provision were generally strong. Behavioural support, such as emotional responsiveness and communication, was weaker in some cases.
  • Suggests parent programmes should focus on emotional and behavioural support, not only provision.

Cases are reported in aggregate only. Consent was taken, and no names, initials or identifying details are shown.

Test Anxiety and Attention Among College Students

Synthesized a comprehensive research paper and literature review analyzing the cognitive impacts of testing stress.

Read the study summary

Question

Is higher test anxiety linked to more attention lapses in college students?

Method

Correlational, cross-sectional study of 203 undergraduates aged 18–25 in Bengaluru, recruited by convenience sampling. They completed an online form with the Westside Test Anxiety Scale (Driscoll, 2007) and the Attention-Related Cognitive Errors Scale (Carriere et al., 2008), after informed consent and with a debrief and counselling information at the end.

Results

  • Test anxiety was moderate (M = 30.4, SD = 4.89), as were attention errors (M = 36.2, SD = 5.85).
  • Attention scores were not normally distributed, so I used Spearman’s correlation: ρ = .272, p < .001.
  • This is a positive but weak link. The null hypothesis was rejected.

Takeaway

Students with higher test anxiety reported more lapses in attention, but anxiety explains only part of the picture. Sleep, study habits and metacognition may matter too. Limits: self-report, convenience sample and a cross-sectional design, so no causal claims. Suggested supports include pre-exam workshops, relaxation and attention training.

Burnout Among Students in Different Academic Programs

Investigated differences in academic burnout across disciplines through quantitative research and data analysis.

Read the study summary

Question

Do students in more demanding programs, such as medicine and nursing, report more burnout than students in less demanding ones, such as business and psychology?

Method

Comparative cross-sectional online survey of 43 students in Bengaluru (23 less demanding, 20 more demanding), using the 12-item Maslach Burnout Inventory–Student Survey (exhaustion, cynicism and academic efficacy). Theory base: Maslach’s burnout model, the Job Demands–Resources model and Conservation of Resources theory.

Results

  • Mean burnout: 38.1 (SD = 4.63) in the more demanding group and 36.5 (SD = 3.96) in the less demanding group.
  • Mann–Whitney U = 184, p = .265, so the difference was not statistically significant.

Takeaway

Burnout looks like a general student concern, not one limited to high-pressure courses. Limits: small convenience sample and self-report. Future work needs larger samples and variables such as coping and self-efficacy.

Neighbourhood Safety and Community Concerns Survey

Surveyed residents’ perceptions of safety and everyday community problems in an urban Bengaluru neighbourhood.

Read the study summary

Method

Quantitative descriptive survey of 20 residents (convenience sample) using the 20-item Neighbourhood Safety and Community Concerns Survey, modelled on the Ashland Community Policing Survey. Items were rated 1 (not at all concerned) to 5 (extremely concerned), on paper, with anonymity assured.

Of 20 issues, 8 rated low concern, 10 moderate and 2 high (trespassing/loitering and noise).

Takeaway

Residents felt relatively safe from serious crime but were more bothered by everyday disorder, in line with Broken Windows Theory and ideas of collective efficacy. Perceived safety shapes wellbeing, so community-focused policing should cover shared spaces, noise and traffic. Limits: 20 people from one area, surveyed in the evening.

Ethics, copyright and legal awareness

APA ethics and professional behaviour

The APA Ethics Code rests on five principles: beneficence and nonmaleficence (do good, avoid harm), fidelity and responsibility, integrity, justice, and respect for people’s rights and dignity. I am a student, not a licensed psychologist, but I follow their spirit in everything I post.

  • Stay accurate: base posts on research and cite it, and never make exaggerated or misleading claims (Standard 5.01, public statements).
  • Know my limits: I work within my training and avoid diagnosing or treating anyone online (Standard 2.01, competence).
  • Public advice is general: online content should be grounded in professional knowledge and must not suggest a therapist–client relationship with the reader (Standard 5.04, media presentations).
  • Avoid harm: I avoid graphic or triggering descriptions and add support information when discussing distress (Standard 3.04).
  • Be honest about who I am: I describe my experience as it is, without overstating my role.
Confidentiality and online boundaries

Confidentiality means protecting what people share with me in a professional or learning setting (APA Standard 4.01). Online, it extends to anything that could identify someone, including names, faces, voices, school or workplace, and unusual combinations of details.

  • No case details: my internship is described only by what I learned, never about the children, families or sessions I observed.
  • Anonymise examples: any scenario I use is fictional or a blend, so nobody can be recognised.
  • Consent first: I do not post photos, quotes or stories of other people without their clear permission, and I do not share private messages or screenshots.
  • Keep boundaries: I do not give personal advice in comments or DMs. I point people to qualified professionals or helplines such as Tele-MANAS (14416).
  • Mind my footprint: I share only the contact details needed for professional contact, because what is posted online can be copied and is hard to remove.
Copyright and Creative Commons

In India, the Copyright Act, 1957 protects original written, artistic and audiovisual work from the moment it is created, with no registration needed. Being easy to find online does not mean it is free to use.

  • What is protected: text, graphics, photos, videos and audio. Facts and ideas are not, but the way they are expressed is.
  • Fair dealing: limited use for research, criticism or review, with credit, is allowed. I still write in my own words and quote sparingly.
  • Creative Commons: CC licences let creators permit reuse. BY requires credit, SA requires sharing under the same terms, NC bars commercial use, and ND bars changes. CC0 means no rights reserved. I check which one applies before reusing anything.
  • Academic honesty: I do not copy from websites or other students, and I cite every source in APA style.
Responsible image sourcing and attribution

Images carry both legal and ethical weight, so I choose them carefully and credit them clearly.

  • Preferred sources: my own work, public-domain material, or openly licensed images (for example Wikimedia Commons or Creative Commons search), always checking the licence terms.
  • Attribute properly: a credit lists title, creator, source and licence, and links to both, written in the references.
  • Photos of people: I use images of identifiable people only with their consent. The photograph on this page is my own.
  • Respectful imagery: I avoid stereotypes and stigmatising pictures of mental illness, and use inclusive, non-sensational visuals.
  • Accessibility: every image has alt text, and text has strong contrast against its background.
Information Technology Act, 2000 (India)

The IT Act gives legal recognition to electronic records and defines cyber offences. These parts matter for anyone who posts or collects information online:

  • Section 43 and 66: unauthorised access to, or damage of, a computer or data, and related offences.
  • Section 66C and 66D: identity theft, and cheating by impersonation online.
  • Section 66E: capturing or sharing someone’s private images without consent is a violation of privacy.
  • Section 67: publishing obscene material electronically is punishable.
  • Section 79: platforms are treated as intermediaries with conditional protection, while individual users stay responsible for what they post.

For this portfolio, that means I never impersonate, access private accounts, or share anyone’s private material. Section 66A was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015, so online speech rules now come from other laws.

Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (India)

The DPDP Act governs how digital personal data is collected and used. It calls the organisation that decides how data is used a Data Fiduciary and the person the data belongs to a Data Principal.

  • Consent: must be free, specific, informed and unambiguous, and can be withdrawn.
  • Purpose limitation and minimisation: collect only what is needed, and use it only for the stated purpose.
  • Rights of the person: to access, correct and erase their data, and to have complaints addressed.
  • Security and accuracy: data must be kept safe and correct, and not stored longer than necessary.
  • Children: data of anyone under 18 needs verifiable parental consent, and tracking or targeted advertising aimed at children is barred.
  • Penalties: serious breaches can attract large financial penalties.

I apply this by publishing only my own email, leaving out my phone number, using no forms, cookies or visitor tracking on this page, and aiming content at adults. If I run a campaign poll, I will keep responses anonymous and collect nothing I do not need.

Reflection

Creating this portfolio taught me that good psychology communication is about translating research into something a tired student can use in thirty seconds, without losing accuracy. I developed skills in visual hierarchy, content planning, source checking and writing for a specific audience, and I learned to treat privacy as a design decision, not an afterthought. The hardest parts were simplifying concepts such as attentional control theory without distorting them, deciding what personal information to leave out, and keeping a creative design readable and accessible.
